Law 3 of 4 · Faraday's law of induction

A changing magnetic field pushes current around a loop

A magnet sitting inside a coil does nothing. Move it, and the needle kicks. What counts is how fast the magnetic flux through the coil changes, and the induced current always pushes back against that change.

Try this

  1. Leave the magnet parked in the middle of the coil. Flux is at its peak, yet the EMF reads zero.
  2. Push it in slowly, then quickly. Same change in flux; the quick push gives a much bigger kick.
  3. Double the turns. The EMF doubles, because each turn adds its own push.
  4. Pull the magnet out instead of pushing it in. The needle swings the other way.

What you're seeing

The blue curve is the flux through the coil. The gold curve is its slope, flipped. EMF is the rate of change of flux, not the flux itself. The glowing arrow in the coil shows the induced field, which always opposes the change that caused it.

Why it matters

Almost all electricity reaching your home is made this way. A turbine spins a magnet past coils, or coils through a field, exactly like the Generator mode. Hydropower stations such as Kulekhani and Upper Tamakoshi in Nepal are giant versions of this bench. Transformers, induction cooktops and wireless chargers use the same law.

Why the minus sign

If the induced current helped the change instead of fighting it, pushing a magnet in slightly would pull it in faster, which would induce more current, forever. That would create energy from nothing. The minus sign, Lenz's law, is energy conservation.
