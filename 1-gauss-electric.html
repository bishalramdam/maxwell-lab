Law 1 of 4 · Gauss's law for electricity

Electric field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge

Wrap any closed surface around some charges. Count the field that pokes out minus the field that pokes in. The total depends only on the charge inside, never on the surface's size or shape.

Try this

  1. With one charge inside, stretch the surface from tiny to huge. The flux reading doesn't move.
  2. Load Charge outside. Lines cross the surface twice, once in and once out, so they cancel.
  3. Load Dipole and enclose both charges. Net flux is zero even though the field inside is strong.
  4. Park a charge exactly on the rim. The flux reads half of it.

What you're measuring

Flux is the amount of field passing through a surface. Where the rim glows red, field is leaving. Where it glows blue, field is entering. Add them up around the whole loop and you get Qenc/ε₀.

Why it matters

This is Coulomb's law rewritten so it works for any shape. Engineers use it to find the field inside coaxial cables, around power lines and between capacitor plates without doing a single messy integral. It also explains why a charged metal box keeps its interior field-free, the idea behind a Faraday cage.

About this simulation

The bench is a flat slice of space. Each charge behaves like a long charged rod seen end-on, so its field falls off as 1/r instead of 1/r². Gauss's law holds exactly in both cases, and the flux reading is computed by integrating E · n̂ around the rim at 720 points.
